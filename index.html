Why an engineering background matters for AI quality

Most people evaluating LLM output today come from one of two places: they either understand models but not systems, or systems but not models. My last year has been spent building the bridge — as the first AI quality function at LeasingMarkt.de, I built the evaluation methodology for the company's AI-powered search from scratch.

That work sits on twelve years of test automation architecture. When an AI response fails an eval, I don't stop at "hallucination detected." I trace it: was it the prompt, the retrieval layer, the data pipeline, or a regression introduced in the last deploy? That's the difference between flagging failures and fixing them.

The patterns I document — recurring hallucinations, contextual inconsistencies, edge-case failures — feed directly back into prompt design and product decisions with engineering and product teams.

// case study

Argus

Automated test monitoring & failure analysis agent

internal platform · unprompted initiative · now standard workflow

One project I'm particularly proud of. We run nightly Playwright tests across multiple browsers and environments, and failures often went unnoticed until engineers manually checked CI logs. I designed Argus to close that gap: it automatically monitors executions, parses Playwright results, identifies failed tests, extracts failure metadata, and publishes structured reports into Slack.

Triage now starts with a hypothesis instead of a blank log file. It began as an unprompted side initiative — it's now part of the team's standard CI/CD workflow.

Next phase: LLM-powered root cause analysis using Claude, to explain failures and suggest fixes automatically. Argus combines quality engineering, automation, observability, and AI-assisted diagnostics — which is exactly where I see AI-driven quality assurance heading.
